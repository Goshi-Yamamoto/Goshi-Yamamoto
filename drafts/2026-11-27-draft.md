# The Increase in Arabica Coffee Prices and Pricing Decisions for Small Cafes in Hawaii

## 1. Introduction

International Arabica coffee prices increased strongly between 2023 and 2025. The annual average price increased from about **$4.54 per kilogram in 2023 to $8.47 in 2025**, an increase of about 87% (World Bank, 2026). This matters for small cafes in Hawaii that use imported Arabica coffee. Cafe owners may face higher costs and need to decide whether to raise prices. Customers are also affected because higher prices may cause them to reduce purchases or choose another cafe.

This paper asks how much a small cafe should change its coffee price when coffee-bean costs increase. I use a simulation model to find the price that maximizes monthly contribution profit under different assumptions about customer price elasticity and coffee-cost pass-through. The model represents a small Hawaii cafe using imported Arabica beans. Hawaiian-grown coffee, including Kona coffee, is excluded because its pricing is different.

## 2. Economic Framework

**Price elasticity of demand** measures how strongly quantity demanded responds to a price change. Demand with an absolute elasticity below 1 is relatively inelastic, while demand above 1 is elastic. Previous estimates vary widely. Hawaii Department of Agriculture (2024) reports coffee-demand elasticities of about -0.08 to -0.54 across importing countries. Koerner (2002) reports about -0.59 to -1.12 for roasted coffee in Germany. Nakamura and Zerom (2009) report median estimates near 1.96 and 3.46 in absolute value under different models. These estimates suggest that demand for a single seller or brand may be more elastic than demand for coffee as a whole.

This difference is important under **monopolistic competition**. If one cafe raises its price while nearby cafes do not, customers may switch to competitors, making its demand more elastic. If many cafes face the same cost increase and also raise prices, the demand faced by one cafe may be less elastic. Food is also a **complementary good**, because losing a coffee customer may also mean losing a food sale. I therefore use **contribution margin**, which subtracts variable costs from revenue. Fixed costs such as rent are excluded. The model also follows **marginal analysis** because profit is highest near the point where marginal revenue and marginal cost are balanced.

## 3. Model and Data

The simulated cafe sells a 12 oz black coffee for **$5.00** and sells **2,000 cups per month** at the baseline. Each cup uses about 19.5 grams of roasted coffee (Specialty Coffee Association, 2017), with a 15% roasting weight loss (Franca et al., 2009). I use **$12 per pound** as the 2023 wholesale roasted-coffee benchmark (Bellwether Coffee, 2026). Green-coffee price changes use World Bank monthly Arabica data. Other variable costs include **$0.22 for a cup and lid** and card fees of **2.6% plus $0.15 per transaction** (Square, n.d.-b). I also assume that 40% of customers buy food (Starbucks Corporation, 2023), at an average price of $5.00 (Square, n.d.-a), with a 40% food-cost rate (QuickStok, n.d.).

The main model uses linear demand through the baseline point of $5.00 and 2,000 cups. I tested elasticity from **-0.5 to -4.0** and pass-through from 0% to 100%. Prices were searched in 1-percentage-point steps. Because the original -20% to +40% range reached a boundary, I expanded it to **-50% to +60%**. A Solver check produced results within 0.5 percentage points of the grid results for the main linear-demand cases (see Appendix C).

## 4. Results

The first hypothesis was that higher Arabica prices would have a relatively small effect on bean cost per cup. The baseline bean cost was about **$0.52 per cup**. At 100% pass-through, the increase was only about **$0.09 per cup**, or 1.8% of the $5.00 selling price. At 50% pass-through, it was about **$0.05**. As Figure 1 shows, even full pass-through remained far below the $0.50 threshold. Therefore, Hypothesis 1 was supported.

The second hypothesis was that elasticity would affect the optimal price more than pass-through. The model showed an **87-percentage-point range** in the optimal price change when elasticity changed from -0.5 to -4.0 at 100% pass-through. In contrast, changing pass-through from 0% to 100% at elasticity -2.0 changed the optimum by only **1 percentage point**. Figure 2 shows that the three pass-through lines almost overlap. At elasticity -0.5, the optimal change was about **+48%**, while at -4.0 it was about **-39%**. The optimal change became negative around elasticity -1.0. Therefore, Hypothesis 2 was also supported.

The four reference strategies gave a more practical comparison. The cost-based increase was only **1.8%**. At elasticity -0.5, the best reference strategy was a 10% increase, which raised monthly contribution profit from about **$10,084 to $10,505**. The model optimum of +48% produced about $11,217. However, at elasticities -1.0, -2.0, and -3.5, **no price increase** was the best of the four reference strategies. The grid model also produced price cuts of about **-27% at -2.0 and -37% at -3.5**, which need additional interpretation.

## 5. Recommendation

The results do not support raising prices automatically just because Arabica bean prices increased. The estimated bean-cost increase was small relative to the $5.00 selling price. Customer response and competitor pricing were much more important.

If nearby cafes also raise prices, demand for one cafe may move toward the more inelastic side of the range. This is consistent with broad-market estimates of about -0.08 to -0.54 reported by Hawaii Department of Agriculture (2024). In that situation, a **small price increase** may be reasonable. The reference-strategy results show that a 10% increase worked well at elasticity -0.5, but not at -1.0 or more elastic cases. Therefore, I would recommend checking competitor prices first and using only a modest increase when demand appears relatively inelastic. If the cafe is raising its price alone, keeping the current price is safer. The model also produced some much larger optimal price changes, which are discussed in the next section.

## 6. Strongest Objection and Response

The strongest objection is that the model produces very large price changes. The base results included about **+48% at elasticity -0.5**, **-27% at -2.0**, and **-37% at -3.5**. These values may not be realistic recommendations for an actual cafe.

Several checks help explain why these results should be treated carefully. First, reducing the food-attach rate from 40% to **25%** still produced about **-22% at -2.0 and -33% at -3.5**, so the food assumption alone did not remove the large discount result (see Appendix A). Second, using the **continuous Solver results**, the lower-price cases implied about **3,071 cups per month at -2.0 and 4,624 cups at -3.5**, compared with the 2,000-cup baseline. A small cafe may not have enough seating or labor to serve that volume. Third, competitors may respond to either a large price cut or a large price increase, which would change the demand faced by the cafe. A large price increase may also cause stronger customer switching than the model predicts.

For these reasons, the very large price increases and decreases should be treated as **extreme model results**, not as direct business recommendations. A more practical strategy is to keep prices stable or make only a modest increase when competitor prices and customer behavior support it.

## 7. Limitations

The model has several limitations. It assumes that new and existing customers have the same probability of buying food. International green-coffee prices do not include all transportation, distribution, and roasting costs faced by a Hawaii cafe. The elasticity values were not measured directly for one small Hawaii cafe, and the $12-per-pound benchmark comes from a 2026 industry source. The constant-elasticity robustness check also produced **no finite optimum for all tested elasticity cases** (see Appendix B). Finally, this research is a simulation rather than direct evidence of actual customer behavior, so the results should be treated as scenarios rather than exact predictions.
